## 2. Literature Review

### 2.0 Introduction to the review

This project sits at the intersection of three research threads that, until recently, have mostly developed in isolation: human-aware robot navigation, the study of spatial formation in human social interaction, and learning-based approaches to robot behaviour acquisition. Human-aware navigation research has produced mature, general-purpose techniques for moving a robot safely and comfortably around people, but has historically treated "the group" as, at best, a set of individual obstacles to avoid rather than a social structure with its own geometry. Work on F-formations and conversational groups, conversely, has produced a rich theoretical and computational account of how people arrange themselves in space when interacting, but this work originates largely in social signal processing and computer vision, and rarely closes the loop with an actual mobile robot's motion planner. A smaller body of work sits specifically at the junction of the two — robots that must approach, rather than merely avoid, a group of people — and it is this smaller, more specific literature that this project's Objective 4 (Behavioural Cloning vs. a rule-based baseline for group-approach behaviour) most directly extends. Running alongside both threads is the literature on Learning from Demonstration and Behavioural Cloning, which supplies the methodological toolkit this project uses to learn approach behaviour directly from the PLUS-HRI dataset's non-expert demonstrations, rather than hand-designing it. This chapter reviews all four areas in turn, states this project's aims and objectives in light of them (Section 2.1), and closes with an explicit statement of the gap this dissertation addresses.

### 2.1 Human-aware and socially aware robot navigation

The foundational framing of this area is Kruse *et al.*'s (2013) survey, which organises human-aware navigation around three axes — comfort, naturalness, and sociability — and catalogues the dominant early techniques: fixed personal-distance thresholds, potential-field and social-force models, and cost-map-based planners. The social-force model itself originates earlier, in Helbing and Molnár's (1995) physics-inspired account of pedestrian dynamics as a system of attractive and repulsive forces, which remains the conceptual ancestor of almost every "keep-your-distance" rule used in social navigation since, including the standoff-distance parameter this project's own rule-based baseline uses. The practical, ROS-native descendant of this line of work is Lu, Hershberger and Smart's (2014) layered-costmap architecture, which is the actual mechanism behind the social costmap layers available in Nav2 — the navigation stack this project runs TIAGo on — and is worth citing precisely because it is not merely theoretical background but the literal software this project's baseline sits on top of.

Two more recent surveys update this picture substantially. Ríos-Martínez, Spalanzani and Laugier (2015) trace the field's evolution from Hall's (1966) original proxemics theory (personal, social, and public distance zones, defined for static human–human interaction) through to its adaptation for moving robots, and are useful precisely for making explicit an assumption this project inherits and must justify: that proxemic distances calibrated on human–human interaction transfer sensibly to human–robot interaction, which is not obviously true and is worth a sentence of critical acknowledgement in this project's Methodology chapter. Mavrogiannis *et al.*'s (2023) "Core Challenges" survey is more recent still and explicitly names *group navigation* — moving with or around a group rather than a single pedestrian — as one of the field's open, comparatively under-solved challenges, which is a strong, recent, citable justification for why this project's specific problem (approaching, not merely avoiding, a group) is a live research gap rather than a solved one. Most directly relevant of all is Singamaneni *et al.*'s (2024) taxonomy survey, which explicitly separates "approaching" from "avoiding" and "accompanying" as distinct social navigation sub-problems and catalogues the (comparatively few) systems that address approach specifically — this is arguably the single most load-bearing citation for framing this project's contribution within the current state of the field, since it is both very recent and taxonomically precise about exactly the sub-problem this project tackles.

**Limitation relative to this project:** taken together, this literature supplies robust general principles (keep a comfortable distance, prefer smooth admissible paths, treat people specially in the costmap) but, with the exception of the approach-specific work reviewed in 2.2, gives no specific method for *selecting where a robot should stop* relative to a multi-person group, nor for combining that decision with a learned rather than hand-designed policy. This project addresses that gap directly.

### 2.2 Proxemics, F-formations, and robot group-approach

The theoretical vocabulary for "where do people stand relative to each other" originates in Kendon's (1990) F-formation system: when people interact in a small, free-standing group, they arrange themselves so that the space between them — the *o-space* — is jointly and exclusively accessible to all members, with a surrounding *p-space* (where members themselves stand) and *r-space* (the outer region from which non-members may observe but not join) forming concentric rings around it. This vocabulary is what this project's own O-space estimation (Phase C) is built on, even in its simplified, mutual-facing-assumption form.

Computationally, Cristani *et al.* (2011) were among the first to formalise F-formation *detection* as an inference problem, using a Hough-voting scheme over inferred head/body orientations to locate o-space centres in still images — this is the direct ancestor of the "vote for where people are facing, then find the convergence point" family of methods. Setti *et al.* (2015) extend this into a more general, robust detection framework (individuating free-standing conversational groups in arbitrary images, without assuming a fixed group size or shape), and remains the most directly cited F-formation detection paper in the proposal. Vascon *et al.* (2016) offer an alternative, game-theoretic formulation using dominant-set clustering, which is worth including specifically because it demonstrates that F-formation detection does not have to depend on reliable *orientation* estimates at all — dominant-set clustering only needs relative position — which is directly relevant to this project's own situation, where (as documented in Phase C) no reliable per-person orientation ground truth exists in the PLUS-HRI dataset at all (`gaze_uniface.csv` is entirely empty), forcing the adoption of the same kind of position-only, mutual-facing fallback that Vascon's method implies is methodologically defensible rather than merely a workaround.

The smaller literature that closes the loop with an actual moving robot is the most directly relevant to Objective 4. Satake *et al.*'s (2009) HRI paper is the foundational work here: a robot approaching a person from a socially acceptable direction based on predicted walking trajectory, establishing that *how* and *from where* a robot approaches measurably affects whether people perceive the interaction as natural or intrusive — this is essentially the single-person predecessor of this project's group-approach problem. Truong and Ngo (2017) extend approach-style reasoning into a proactive social-motion model for dynamic, crowded environments, folding predicted human motion into the approach trajectory rather than treating the scene as static. Repiso, Garrell and Sanfeliu (2019) address the closely related side-by-side approach-and-accompany problem, adapting an approach trajectory in real time as people themselves move — useful for this project mainly as a demonstration that "approach" is not typically treated as a single static goal pose in the wider literature, which is worth an honest limitation note: this project's own baseline and BC model *do* treat the approach target as a single static pose (x, y, yaw) rather than a dynamically-updated one, a simplification made necessary by the offline demonstration dataset it learns from, and worth stating plainly rather than leaving implicit.

Most directly relevant of all is Yang *et al.*'s (2021) PLOS ONE paper, which released a dataset explicitly of human and robot approach behaviours into small free-standing conversational groups — the closest published analogue to this project's own goal. The key, citable distinction (already identified correctly in this project's proposal) is that Yang *et al.*'s dataset provides direct group/approach-pose annotations, collected under controlled conditions, whereas the PLUS-HRI dataset used here provides none of that structure natively — this project must derive group and O-space labels itself, from raw non-expert teleoperation video, via its own detection and clustering pipeline (Phases B and C), which is both a harder starting point and, arguably, a more realistic one for eventual real-world deployment, where clean pre-annotated group structure will never be available online.

**Limitation relative to this project:** F-formation theory and detection methods give a rigorous account of group *geometry*, and the approach-specific robotics literature shows that approach direction and distance measurably matter to social acceptability — but none of the reviewed work combines F-formation-derived approach-pose targets with a *learned*, demonstration-trained policy; the closest analogues either hand-design the approach rule (Satake, Truong & Ngo, Repiso et al.) or provide a clean annotated dataset rather than requiring the group structure to be inferred from raw non-expert demonstration footage (Yang et al.). This project's combination — infer group structure from imperfect data, then learn (rather than hand-design) the approach policy from non-expert demonstrations, evaluated against a rule-based baseline — is not addressed by any single one of these.

### 2.3 Learning from Demonstration and Behavioural Cloning

Argall *et al.*'s (2009) survey remains the standard reference point for Learning from Demonstration (LfD) as a field, establishing the core taxonomy (how demonstrations are recorded, how the mapping from state to action is learned, and how the "correspondence problem" between demonstrator and robot embodiment is handled) that essentially every subsequent LfD paper, including this one's own project structure, is implicitly organised around. Ravichandar *et al.*'s (2020) more recent survey updates this picture for the deep-learning era, covering how neural architectures have changed what LfD can represent (from simple regression to full end-to-end policies) and explicitly flagging *data efficiency* — how much demonstration data a method needs before it generalises — as one of the field's persistent open problems; this is directly relevant to this project's own finding that 462 independent demonstration events, however many rows they expand to, is a genuinely small number by the standards this survey discusses, and is the most defensible explanation on record for why this project's Behavioural Cloning model plateaus rather than continuing to improve with further tuning.

Behavioural Cloning specifically — treating demonstration-following as supervised learning from state to action — traces back to Pomerleau's (1989) ALVINN system, one of the earliest applications of a neural network to autonomous steering from raw sensor input via direct imitation of a human driver, and is worth citing as the origin point of the entire "learn to imitate the demonstrator's actions directly" paradigm this project's `train_baseline_model.py`/`train_approach_pose_model.py` scripts sit within, thirty-five years later. The best-known limitation of naive Behavioural Cloning — that small prediction errors compound over time because the model only ever sees demonstrator-visited states during training, then drifts into unfamiliar states during deployment — was formalised by Ross, Gordon and Bagnell (2011), whose DAgger algorithm addresses it by iteratively querying the demonstrator on the learner's own visited states. This is directly relevant as a **stated limitation, not an implemented fix**: this project's Behavioural Cloning model is trained via plain offline Behavioural Cloning on fixed, pre-recorded demonstrations, with no DAgger-style online correction loop, which is a legitimate and common simplification for a Masters-scale project but should be named explicitly as a source of the distributional-shift risk this project's own live-simulation results (rather than its offline evaluation numbers) are most likely to expose.

**Limitation relative to this project:** the LfD/BC literature is mature on general principles (Argall, Ravichandar) and honest about its central weakness (Ross, Gordon and Bagnell's compounding-error problem), but says nothing project-specific about *social* group-approach behaviour as a target task, nor about learning from a dataset recorded by genuinely non-expert operators rather than curated demonstrators. This project's contribution is applying the BC paradigm to exactly that harder, messier setting, and evaluating — rather than assuming — whether it beats a simple geometric rule on the specific task of group approach.

### 2.4 Supporting perception literature

Two further threads support the pipeline rather than defining its research question, but still warrant a critical citation each rather than being treated as mere tool references. Person detection in this project relies on YOLOv8, whose lineage runs back to Redmon *et al.*'s (2016) original "You Only Look Once" formulation of object detection as a single-pass regression problem rather than the slower two-stage detect-then-classify pipelines that preceded it; the specific YOLOv8 architecture used here (rather than the original 2016 design) is documented in Yaseen's (2024) independent architectural analysis, since Ultralytics did not publish a formal peer-reviewed paper alongside the YOLOv8 release itself — worth stating plainly in the Methodology chapter as a minor provenance quirk rather than an oversight. This project's own validated 99.7% recall figure (Phase B) sits comfortably within, and is directly comparable to, the general real-time-detection literature this lineage represents. The proposal's named perception model, NVIDIA's LocateAnything-3B (Wang *et al.*, 2026), is a very recent open-vocabulary vision-language grounding model using parallel box decoding rather than YOLO's grid-based regression; this project's own head-to-head comparison against YOLOv8n (documented in Phase B, 9 August 2026) is itself a small primary contribution worth foregrounding in the Results & Discussion chapter, since a directly measured speed/accuracy comparison between a general-purpose grounding model and a specialised real-time detector, on cluttered, robot-mounted-camera indoor HRI footage, does not yet exist in the published literature this review could locate.

Finally, the navigation and platform layers this project runs on both have citable, primary-source documentation rather than only vendor pages: Macenski *et al.*'s (2020) "Marathon 2" paper is the actual system paper behind ROS 2's Nav2 stack, and Pagès, Marchionni and Ferro's (2016) paper introduces the TIAGo platform itself. Both are worth a brief citation in the Methodology or Implementation chapter (not the Literature Review proper) when TIAGo/Nav2 are first introduced as the experimental platform, since citing the platform's own system paper rather than only a marketing page is standard academic practice and an easy, low-effort mark to pick up.

> Taken together, the reviewed literature establishes general principles of human-aware navigation (Kruse et al., 2013; Ríos-Martínez, Spalanzani and Laugier, 2015) and explicitly names group-specific approach behaviour as an under-addressed sub-problem within it (Mavrogiannis et al., 2023; Singamaneni et al., 2024); formal, computational tools for representing group structure that do not depend on unreliable orientation estimates (Kendon, 1990; Vascon et al., 2016); a small robotics literature that hand-designs approach behaviour for single people or dynamically-tracked groups (Satake et al., 2009; Truong and Ngo, 2017; Repiso, Garrell and Sanfeliu, 2019) or provides a cleanly annotated group-approach dataset distinct from this project's own (Yang et al., 2021); and a mature Learning from Demonstration literature that supplies both the Behavioural Cloning paradigm this project uses (Pomerleau, 1989) and an honest account of its central weakness (Ross, Gordon and Bagnell, 2011), alongside a documented, data-efficiency-driven ceiling on how much a small demonstration set can be expected to teach it (Ravichandar et al., 2020). This is the gap this dissertation addresses, and Chapter 5 reports what happens when it is: a split, honestly-measured result in which the learned model beats the rule on orientation but not on position, and neither reaches a fully socially-acceptable orientation accuracy on this dataset — itself a finding consistent with, rather than contradicting, the data-efficiency limits this chapter has already flagged.
