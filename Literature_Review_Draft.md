## 2. Literature Review

### 2.0 Introduction to the review

This project sits at the intersection of four research threads that, until quite recently, mostly developed in isolation from one another. The oldest of the four — proxemics and the study of human spatial behaviour — is a mid-twentieth-century anthropological theory that predates robotics entirely, but supplies the vocabulary (personal space, social distance, the F-formation) that every later thread borrows without alteration. Human-aware and socially aware robot navigation is the applied engineering literature that grew out of trying to make mobile robots respect that vocabulary, and it has matured into a field with its own surveys, its own software (the social costmap layers this project's own baseline sits on top of), and its own open problems. A third thread, running mostly in computer vision and social-signal processing rather than robotics, is concerned with *detecting* the group structures proxemic theory describes computationally, from images or video, without necessarily doing anything about them once found. The fourth thread — Learning from Demonstration and, specifically, Behavioural Cloning — is the machine-learning toolkit this project uses to acquire approach behaviour from data rather than hand-designing it, together with a smaller and more sobering literature on exactly why Behavioural Cloning is a harder proposition than it first appears.

A fifth, narrower thread sits at the direct intersection of the second and third: a small number of robotics papers that treat *approaching* a person or a group, rather than merely avoiding one, as the object of study. This is the most specific literature this project extends, and it is reviewed in its own right (Section 2.4) rather than folded into the general navigation survey material, because it is the closest published analogue to Objective 4's actual comparison. Finally, because this project's own final results (a 60-trial live simulation, reported in Chapter 5) rest on a specific choice of learning algorithms and a specific statistical treatment of a necessarily small experiment, two further short sections are added that were not present in earlier drafts of this review: one grounding the actual supervised-learning methods used for the approach-pose model in their own primary literature (Section 2.6), and one grounding the experimental and statistical methodology used to evaluate a twelve-condition, ten-trial-per-condition simulation study (Section 2.9). Earlier drafts of this review cited Random Forest and treated `scikit-learn`'s regressors as though they were self-evidently correct tools; a properly rigorous review does not do that, and this one does not.

The chapter proceeds as follows. Section 2.1 reviews proxemics and F-formation theory — the theoretical foundation everything else in this dissertation ultimately rests on. Section 2.2 reviews human-aware and socially aware robot navigation as a field. Section 2.3 reviews the computational literature on detecting group structure from sensor data. Section 2.4 reviews the specific, narrower robotics literature on approaching people and groups. Section 2.5 reviews Learning from Demonstration and Behavioural Cloning, including its known failure modes. Section 2.6 reviews the supervised-learning methods (Random Forest, gradient boosting) this project actually trains. Section 2.7 reviews the supporting perception literature (person detection and vision-language grounding). Section 2.8 reviews the platform and navigation-stack literature (TIAGo, Nav2). Section 2.9 reviews the experimental-methodology and statistics literature that grounds how this project's live simulation is designed and analysed. Section 2.10 summarises all of it against this project's own contribution and states the research gap explicitly. Section 2.11 revisits this project's aims and objectives in light of the review.

### 2.1 Proxemics and F-formation Theory

The theoretical starting point for everything in this dissertation is Hall's (1966) *The Hidden Dimension*, the foundational text of proxemics — the study of how humans structure and use the physical space around them as a form of non-verbal communication. Hall's central, empirically-derived claim is that interpersonal distance is not continuous but organised into discrete zones with different social meanings: intimate distance (roughly 0–0.45 m), personal distance (0.45–1.2 m), social distance (1.2–3.6 m), and public distance (beyond 3.6 m), each appropriate to a different kind of relationship and activity. This is not a minor footnote citation for this project: the personal-distance boundary is the literal numeric basis for the 0.7 m person-clearance and 0.45–1.2 m proxemic band this project's own evaluation metrics use directly (`Section_3_6_Evaluation_Metrics.md` names Hall's personal zone explicitly when defining the minimum-distance-to-person metric), and Hall's zone theory is also the origin of Helbing and Molnár's (1995) social-force model, reviewed in Section 2.2, which formalises "keep your distance" as a repulsive force rather than a discrete zone boundary. It is worth being honest about a limitation of Hall's work that this project inherits rather than resolves: Hall's zones were derived from observing human–human interaction in a specific American cultural context in the 1960s, and every one of this project's own distance thresholds — the 0.7 m clearance, the 0.5–2.0 m task-success band, the personal-distance framing of the minimum-distance metric — assumes without independent verification that those human–human norms transfer sensibly to human–robot interaction and to a 2020s international student population. Ríos-Martínez, Spalanzani and Laugier (2015), reviewed in the next section, name this same transfer assumption explicitly as one the wider field has not resolved either, which is some comfort but not a fix.

Kendon's (1990) *Conducting Interaction: Patterns of Behavior in Focused Encounters* takes Hall's zone theory and gives it a specifically *social* rather than purely spatial reading: when people interact in a small, free-standing group — what Kendon calls an F-formation — they do not merely maintain distance from one another, they jointly construct and defend a shared, mutually accessible space at the centre of the group, which Kendon terms the **o-space**. This o-space is ringed by the **p-space** (the narrow band of floor each participant physically occupies) and, beyond that, the **r-space** (the outer region from which a non-participant may observe the group without being considered part of it). Kendon's vocabulary is not an optional flourish in this dissertation — it is the literal name of the module this project builds. Every reference in this project's own code and documentation to "the O-space," to "P-space slots," and to a robot "intruding on" a group's O-space is a direct, named application of Kendon's 1990 theory: `Section_3_6_Evaluation_Metrics.md` cites Kendon by name when defining the O-space intrusion metric, and the final live-simulation evaluation (`APPROACH_ACCURACY_RESULTS.md`) scores every trial's approach accuracy against **twelve ideal standing slots derived directly from F-formation geometry** — one slot on the bisector of each formation gap wider than 45°, placed 0.6 m outside the group's O-space, facing the group centre — which is Kendon's theory operationalised as a scoring rubric, not merely cited as background. This is, if anything, a stronger and more explicit dependence on a single 1990 theory text than the previous draft of this review conveyed, and it is worth stating plainly: without Kendon's F-formation model, this project's own headline result (Table 1 of `APPROACH_ACCURACY_RESULTS.md`) would have no principled definition of "correct" to measure against at all.

A necessary limitation, stated by this project's own documentation and repeated here rather than softened: Kendon's original framework assumes the observer can determine each participant's *orientation*, since o-space, p-space and r-space are all defined relative to where people are facing, not merely where they are standing. As Section 2.3 and Section 3.3 (Methodology) both document, this project's own dataset — the PLUS-HRI recordings — contains no usable orientation ground truth at all (`gaze_uniface.csv` is entirely empty across every session that has the column), and the live Gazebo actor models used for the final 60-trial evaluation do not encode gaze either (`Section_3_6_Evaluation_Metrics.md`, §3.6.5, states this explicitly: "O-space radii are estimated from the spatial extent of the group members rather than observed from the participants' orientations, since Gazebo actor models do not encode gaze"). This project's entire treatment of F-formations, from the very first offline clustering script to the final 60-trial live result, is therefore built on a **documented, repeatedly-stated departure from Kendon's original orientation-dependent construction**, substituting a mutual-facing/position-only approximation throughout. Section 2.3 reviews the specific line of computer-vision literature (Vascon et al., 2016) that this substitution leans on for its methodological defensibility.

### 2.2 Human-Aware and Socially Aware Robot Navigation

The applied engineering literature that grew from proxemic theory is best entered through Kruse *et al.*'s (2013) survey, which organises the field around three criteria a socially competent navigating robot should satisfy — comfort, naturalness, and sociability — and catalogues the dominant early techniques for achieving them: fixed personal-distance thresholds derived more or less directly from Hall, potential-field and social-force models, and cost-map-based planners that penalise proximity to people rather than forbidding it outright. The social-force model itself is older than Kruse *et al.*'s survey and traces to Helbing and Molnár's (1995) physics-inspired account of pedestrian dynamics as a system of attractive and repulsive forces between agents; this remains the conceptual ancestor of essentially every "keep-your-distance" rule used in social navigation since, including the standoff-distance parameter this project's own rule-based baseline hard-codes at 1.2 m — chosen, as `V2_RETRAINING_STUDY.md` and the project's own Methodology chapter note, to sit at Hall's own personal/social boundary rather than as an arbitrary tuning constant. The practical, ROS-native descendant of this line of work is Lu, Hershberger and Smart's (2014) layered-costmap architecture, which is not background reading for this project but the literal software mechanism behind the social costmap layers available in Nav2 (Macenski *et al.*, 2020, Section 2.8), the navigation stack this project runs TIAGo on.

Three more recent surveys substantially update and complicate this picture, and each is relevant to this project for a different, specific reason rather than as generic field-scoping. Ríos-Martínez, Spalanzani and Laugier (2015) trace the field's evolution from Hall's original, static, human–human proxemics through to its adaptation for moving robots, and are the source (already flagged in Section 2.1) for the explicit, unresolved concern that proxemic norms calibrated on human–human interaction may not transfer cleanly to human–robot interaction — a concern this project inherits directly and has not independently tested, since its own evaluation is conducted entirely against simulated actors rather than real human bystanders. Mavrogiannis *et al.*'s (2023) "Core Challenges" survey is more recent still and explicitly names **group navigation** — moving with, around, or toward a group rather than a single pedestrian — as one of the field's comparatively under-solved open challenges, which gives this project's chosen problem a direct, recent, citable justification as a live research gap rather than a solved one. Most directly relevant of all is Singamaneni *et al.*'s (2024) taxonomy survey, which is unusual among social-navigation surveys in explicitly separating **approaching** from **avoiding** and **accompanying** as three taxonomically distinct social-navigation sub-problems, and in cataloguing the comparatively small number of systems that address approach specifically. This is arguably the single most load-bearing citation in the entire review for framing exactly what this project contributes, because it is both very recent (2024) and taxonomically precise about the exact sub-problem — approach, not avoidance, not accompaniment — that this project's final 60-trial result is actually measuring.

A critical point worth making explicitly, which the earlier draft of this review did not make sharply enough: **almost none of this survey literature distinguishes position accuracy from orientation accuracy as separately achievable objectives.** Kruse *et al.* (2013), Ríos-Martínez, Spalanzani and Laugier (2015), Mavrogiannis *et al.* (2023) and Singamaneni *et al.* (2024) all discuss "comfort" and "naturalness" as unified, holistic properties of a trajectory or a stopping behaviour, rather than as a pair of quantities that might respond differently to the same intervention. This project's own final result decisively falsifies any assumption that they must move together: Table 1 of `APPROACH_ACCURACY_RESULTS.md` shows the tuned MLP policy achieving the best position accuracy of any policy tested (a median of 8.5 cm from an ideal F-formation slot under YOLOv8n) while simultaneously being the **worst** of all three policies on orientation accuracy (79.1° mean error, worse even than the untrained naive baseline's implicit facing behaviour) — a genuine, measured dissociation between "where to stand" and "which way to face" that the reviewed navigation literature's holistic framing does not anticipate or provide any vocabulary for discussing. This is not a criticism levelled at the surveys for its own sake; it is the review correctly identifying, in advance of Chapter 5, exactly where this project's contribution sits relative to the field's existing conceptual toolkit.

**Limitation of this literature relative to the project:** taken together, this body of work supplies robust general principles — keep a comfortable distance, prefer smooth admissible paths, treat people specially in the costmap, and (per the two most recent surveys) recognise approach as its own distinct problem — but, with the exception of the narrower literature reviewed in Section 2.4, it gives no specific method for *selecting where a robot should stop* relative to a multi-person group, no treatment of position and orientation accuracy as separable outcomes, and no guidance on combining that decision with a learned rather than hand-designed policy. This project addresses the first and third gaps directly and, as an unplanned but genuine secondary contribution, surfaces the second.

### 2.3 Computational Detection of Group Structure

Where Section 2.1 covers the *theory* of F-formations and Section 2.2 covers robots that *navigate around* people generally, this section covers a distinct, third literature: computer-vision and social-signal-processing work whose object is to *detect* F-formations and conversational groups automatically from sensor data, typically without any robot or any navigation decision involved at all. This project draws on this literature for its own clustering and O-space estimation pipeline, and — critically — for the methodological justification of the position-only approximation forced on it by its dataset's lack of orientation data (Section 2.1).

Cristani *et al.* (2011) were among the first to formalise F-formation *detection* as a statistical inference problem, using a Hough-voting scheme over inferred head and body orientations to locate the convergence point — the o-space centre — in still images. This is the direct methodological ancestor of the entire "vote for where people are facing, then find where the votes converge" family of detection methods, but it depends, like Kendon's original theory, on a usable orientation estimate for every person in the scene. Setti *et al.* (2015) extend this into a more general and more robust detection framework capable of individuating free-standing conversational groups of arbitrary, unknown size and shape in unconstrained images, but retains the same orientation dependency as Cristani *et al.* Vascon *et al.* (2016) break with both by proposing a game-theoretic formulation using dominant-set clustering that recovers F-formation structure from relative **position alone**, explicitly *not* requiring any orientation estimate at all. This is the single most consequential citation in this section for the project's own methodology: it is the direct, independent, peer-reviewed justification for the mutual-facing, position-only fallback this project's own `cluster_groups.py` and `estimate_approach_points.py` scripts implement in place of Kendon's orientation-dependent original construction, and it converts what would otherwise look like an ad-hoc workaround, forced by a defective dataset, into a defensible instance of an established alternative methodology.

A fourth, more recent addition to this literature — not present in the previous draft of this review — is Swofford *et al.*'s (2020) DANTE (Deep Affinity Network for clustering conversational interactants), which reframes F-formation detection as a deep, learned pairwise-affinity clustering problem: rather than hand-designing a distance-and-orientation rule (as Cristani, Setti, and Vascon all do, in their different ways) or a fixed similarity threshold (as this project's own `GROUP_DISTANCE_THRESHOLD`-based clustering does), DANTE trains a neural network to predict, for every pair of detected people, the probability that they belong to the same conversational group, and then clusters on the learned affinity graph rather than a hand-set geometric rule. Its inclusion here earns its place on a genuinely critical rather than merely additive basis: DANTE demonstrates that group-detection accuracy can be improved substantially over hand-designed distance thresholds precisely by *learning* the affinity function from labelled data — which is exactly the kind of improvement this project's own clustering stage never attempts, because no labelled group-affinity dataset exists for the PLUS-HRI recordings and building one was judged out of scope. This is a properly stated limitation rather than an oversight: **this project's own group detection is entirely rule-based** (a single normalised-distance threshold), at precisely the stage of the pipeline where the most recent literature (Swofford *et al.*, 2020) shows a learned alternative measurably outperforms one. Only the downstream approach-*pose* decision is learned in this project; the upstream group-*detection* decision is not, and DANTE is the citation that makes clear this was a choice with a real, literature-documented alternative, not the only available option.

**Limitation of this literature relative to the project:** F-formation detection methods, hand-designed or learned, give increasingly sophisticated accounts of *where a group is and how it is arranged*, but none of the four papers reviewed here connects that detection to a *robot's own approach decision* at all — Cristani, Setti, Vascon and Swofford all operate on static or pre-recorded imagery with no embodied agent in the loop. The bridge from "a group is detected here, with this shape" to "a robot should therefore stop *there*, facing *this* way" is not addressed by this literature; it is addressed only by the narrower robotics literature reviewed next.

### 2.4 Robots That Approach: From Single People to Groups

This is the smallest and most specific literature reviewed in this chapter, and the one this project's Objective 4 most directly extends. Satake *et al.*'s (2009) HRI paper is the foundational work: a robot approaching a single walking person from a socially acceptable direction, predicted from the person's own trajectory, established for the first time in a controlled robotics study that *how* and *from where* a robot approaches measurably affects whether the interaction is perceived as natural or intrusive. This is, in effect, the single-person predecessor of this project's entire group-approach problem, and it establishes the basic experimental logic — vary the approach behaviour, hold everything else fixed, measure the human response or a proxy for it — that this project's own rule-vs-learned comparison inherits, thirteen years and one added social dimension (the group) later. Truong and Ngo (2017) extend approach-style reasoning into a proactive social-motion model for dynamic, crowded environments, explicitly folding predicted human motion into the approach trajectory rather than treating the scene as static — a refinement this project's own rule-based baseline does not attempt, since its approach target (a fixed F-formation slot) is recomputed reactively on each perception update rather than predicted forward in time.

Repiso, Garrell and Sanfeliu (2020) address the closely related side-by-side approach-and-accompany problem: a robot that not only approaches a person or a small group but continuously re-plans its position as people themselves move, maintaining a socially appropriate relative pose throughout an extended interaction rather than treating "approach" as a single one-off event. This is the most useful single citation in this section for stating one of this project's own clearest limitations honestly: Repiso, Garrell and Sanfeliu's system demonstrates, in real robot trials, that a socially competent approach is not generally a static goal pose computed once and then executed — it is (or ought to be) a continuously-updated target. This project's own baseline and both learned policies **do** treat the approach target as a single, static pose (Δx, Δy, Δyaw) computed relative to the robot's current position, recomputed on each new perception update but never explicitly modelled as a moving target with its own dynamics. This is a simplification made necessary by the nature of the PLUS-HRI demonstration dataset, which records completed, discrete approach *events* rather than a continuous accompanying interaction, and it is worth stating as a direct, literature-grounded limitation rather than leaving it implicit.

Most directly relevant of all to this project's specific research question is Yang *et al.*'s (2021) PLOS ONE paper, which released a dataset explicitly of human and robot approach behaviours into small free-standing conversational groups — the closest published analogue anywhere in the literature to this project's own goal. The single most important, and most favourable to this project, distinction between Yang *et al.*'s dataset and the PLUS-HRI dataset used here is one this project's own final results now allow to be stated with more force than the earlier draft of this review could manage: Yang *et al.*'s dataset provides direct, clean, purpose-collected group and approach-pose annotations, whereas PLUS-HRI provides none of that structure at all, forcing this project to build its own detection, clustering, and O-space estimation pipeline from raw, non-expert, retrospectively-repurposed teleoperation video before a single approach pose could even be labelled. The `V2_RETRAINING_STUDY.md` investigation carried out on this project — which found that the true independent sample size behind the offline dataset was not the reported 70,555 rows but a mere 462 (later re-derived as 182) genuinely independent approach *events*, and that this small, noisy corpus imposes a measured "label ambiguity floor" of roughly 0.505 m below which no amount of model capacity or hyperparameter tuning can push the position error — is precisely the cost of not having a dataset like Yang *et al.*'s. This is not a hypothetical drawback stated for form's sake; it is now a directly measured, quantified consequence, reported in this project's own documentation, of choosing a harder, more realistic, non-purpose-built dataset over a cleaner one.

**Limitation of this literature relative to the project:** the approach-specific robotics literature shows convincingly that approach direction, distance, and continuous re-planning all measurably matter to social acceptability — but every system reviewed here **hand-designs** its approach rule (Satake *et al.*, Truong and Ngo, Repiso, Garrell and Sanfeliu all fall into this category), and the one dataset closest to this project's own goal (Yang *et al.*) exists to support future work rather than itself proposing a learned policy. None of the four combines a *learned*, demonstration-trained approach policy with a group (rather than single-person) target, evaluated head-to-head against a hand-designed rule on the same task under the same conditions. That specific combination — which this project's own 60-trial result now delivers in full, including the finding that the learned policy wins decisively on position while losing decisively on orientation — is not addressed anywhere in this literature, and is the central empirical contribution of this dissertation.

### 2.5 Learning from Demonstration and Behavioural Cloning

Argall *et al.*'s (2009) survey remains the standard reference point for Learning from Demonstration (LfD) as a field, establishing a core taxonomy — how demonstrations are recorded, how the mapping from observed state to action is learned, and how the "correspondence problem" between a human demonstrator's embodiment and a robot's is handled — that essentially every subsequent LfD paper, including this project's own structure, is implicitly organised around. Ravichandar *et al.*'s (2020) more recent survey updates this picture for the deep-learning era and explicitly flags **data efficiency** — how much demonstration data a method needs before it generalises — as one of the field's persistent, unresolved open problems. This project's own `V2_RETRAINING_STUDY.md` gives this abstract concern a startlingly concrete, measured answer specific to its own dataset: a systematic sweep (§5a of that study) found position error falling monotonically and predictably as independent training events increased from 120 to 578, extrapolated to project that reaching the proposal's own 0.4 m accuracy threshold would require substantially more independent demonstration data than the entire 24-session PLUS-HRI corpus contains. Ravichandar *et al.*'s survey names data efficiency as a problem in the abstract; this project's own study is, in effect, a single-project case study that quantifies exactly how binding that problem is in practice.

Behavioural Cloning specifically — treating demonstration-following as ordinary supervised learning from state to action, with no explicit model of the demonstrator's own decision process — traces to Pomerleau's (1989) ALVINN system, one of the earliest applications of a neural network to autonomous steering learned by direct imitation of a human driver. Bojarski *et al.*'s (2016) NVIDIA paper, "End to End Learning for Self-Driving Cars," is the modern, large-scale descendant of exactly the same idea — a convolutional network mapping raw camera pixels directly to steering commands, with no hand-engineered intermediate representation — and its inclusion in this draft (it was not present in the previous version of this review) matters because it demonstrates that the core Behavioural Cloning paradigm this project applies to social robot navigation is not a historical curiosity confined to 1989 but a technique that scaled successfully, twenty-seven years later, to a considerably harder real-world control problem. The line from Pomerleau (1989) through Bojarski *et al.* (2016) to this project's own `train_approach_pose_model.py` and `train_approach_pose_mlp.py` is a single, continuous, thirty-five-year methodological lineage, and stating it explicitly is more accurate than treating Behavioural Cloning as though it appeared from nowhere in this project's Methodology chapter.

The best-known limitation of naive Behavioural Cloning — that small prediction errors compound over time because the model only ever sees demonstrator-visited states during training, then drifts into unfamiliar states during autonomous deployment — was formalised by Ross, Gordon and Bagnell (2011), whose DAgger algorithm addresses it by iteratively querying the demonstrator on the learner's own visited states during training. This project's Behavioural Cloning models are trained via plain offline Behavioural Cloning with no DAgger-style online correction loop of any kind — the "expert" here is a fixed corpus of 24 already-recorded, non-expert teleoperation sessions, not an interactive supervisor who could be queried live — and this is stated as a deliberate, named limitation rather than an oversight. Two further, more targeted papers on exactly this weakness were added to this draft after a fuller reading of the completed project's own results, because they turn out to describe this project's own failure mode with unusual precision. Torabi, Warnell and Stone's (2018) "Behavioral Cloning from Observation" is included here specifically for its clear, influential restatement of *why* naive Behavioural Cloning struggles when the available demonstrations do not densely cover the state space the learner will actually encounter — precisely the situation this project's own `V2_RETRAINING_STUDY.md` diagnoses when it finds that the original ("v1") training labels were derived almost exclusively from the terminal, final-half-metre phase of each demonstrated approach, leaving the model with almost no coverage of what a demonstrator does two or three metres out. Codevilla *et al.*'s (2019) "Exploring the Limitations of Behavior Cloning for Autonomous Driving" is the single most directly analogous paper in the entire reference list to this project's own retraining study: using a substantially larger driving dataset than this project has access to, Codevilla *et al.* show empirically that naive Behavioural Cloning's generalisation is bottlenecked less by model architecture than by dataset coverage and the presence of high-variance, causally-confounded "spurious correlation" features — a finding that maps almost exactly onto this project's own §5c/§5d discovery (via a k-nearest-neighbours label-disagreement analysis) that its own training rows carry an intrinsic **label ambiguity floor of roughly 0.505 m**, meaning the target pose is only weakly determined by the available features regardless of which learning algorithm is used to fit them. Citing Codevilla *et al.* here is not decorative: it independently corroborates, from a completely different domain and a completely different research group, this project's own most important negative methodological finding — that no amount of additional model capacity or hyperparameter search can close a gap that is fundamentally a property of the data, not the learner (a claim this project's own hyperparameter study, Section 2.6, tests directly and confirms).

**Limitation of this literature relative to the project:** the LfD/Behavioural Cloning literature is mature on general principles (Argall, Ravichandar) and increasingly honest about the paradigm's central structural weaknesses (Ross, Gordon and Bagnell; Torabi, Warnell and Stone; Codevilla *et al.*), but none of it addresses *social* group-approach behaviour as a target task, and none of it is set in a domain where the demonstrations themselves are recorded by genuinely non-expert operators using a dataset never designed for the research question being asked of it. This project's specific contribution is applying the Behavioural Cloning paradigm to exactly that harder, messier setting, and — critically — using the diagnostic tools this literature provides (compounding error, label ambiguity, dataset-coverage limits) to explain, quantitatively and specifically, *why* its own learned models behave the way they do, rather than reporting a bare accuracy number and moving on.

### 2.6 Supervised Learning Methods Used for the Approach-Pose Model

This section did not exist in the previous draft of this review, and its absence was a genuine gap: a dissertation that trains and reports results from a Random Forest, a Multi-Layer Perceptron, and (in its rejected "v2" ablation study) a gradient-boosted ensemble owes each of those algorithms its own primary citation, in the same way Behavioural Cloning itself is grounded in Pomerleau (1989) rather than merely named. Breiman's (2001) original Random Forest paper is the citation this project's `train_approach_pose_model.py` and `grid_search_approach_pose.py` scripts have been implicitly relying on throughout without ever citing directly: Breiman's method — an ensemble of decision trees, each trained on a bootstrap-resampled subset of the data with a randomised subset of features considered at each split — is exactly the algorithm scikit-learn's `RandomForestRegressor` implements, and it is this project's single best-performing offline policy on orientation accuracy (Table 5.1: 25.78° mean error, tuned) and, in the final systematic 360-model hyperparameter search (`V2_RETRAINING_STUDY.md`, §5d), one of three model families that independently converged on the same performance ceiling, an important piece of corroborating evidence discussed further below.

Friedman's (2001) "Greedy Function Approximation: A Gradient Boosting Machine" is the foundational paper behind gradient-boosted tree ensembles generally, and — via Ke *et al.*'s (2017) LightGBM paper, which introduced the histogram-based binning strategy that scikit-learn's `HistGradientBoostingRegressor` directly adopts — is the specific lineage behind the gradient-boosting model this project trains as part of its rejected "v2" ablation study (`scripts/train_evaluate_v2.py`). Both citations earn their place here on a genuinely evidential basis rather than as an obligatory nod to a library import: the 360-model hyperparameter search reported in `V2_RETRAINING_STUDY.md` §5d found that Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and a Multi-Layer Perceptron — three model families with essentially no shared architectural assumptions, one an ensemble of independently-fit trees, one an ensemble of sequentially-corrected trees, and one a differentiable neural network — all converged, independently, to within 0.009 m of one another on held-out position error after exhaustive tuning. This convergence is itself an important, citable methodological result, and it depends on the reader understanding that these are three genuinely different learning principles (Breiman's bagging-and-feature-randomisation versus Friedman's sequential residual-fitting versus gradient-descent-trained neural weights) rather than three superficially different names for the same idea. That three unrelated algorithms hit the same wall is much stronger evidence that the wall is in the *data* — the label-ambiguity floor discussed in Section 2.5 — than any single algorithm's result could be alone, and this section exists to make sure that argument is properly grounded rather than asserted.

**Limitation of this literature relative to the project:** none of Breiman (2001), Friedman (2001) or Ke *et al.* (2017) has anything to say about social robotics, group approach, or Behavioural Cloning specifically — they are general-purpose supervised-learning methods papers, cited here purely for methodological grounding rather than domain relevance. That is precisely their role in this review: to establish that the algorithms compared in this project's own hyperparameter study are principled, independently-justified choices rather than an arbitrary shortlist, so that the finding that all three agree is meaningful rather than coincidental.

### 2.7 Supporting Perception Literature

Two further threads support the perception pipeline this project's Behavioural Cloning model and rule-based baseline both depend on, without themselves defining the project's research question. Person detection in this project relies on YOLOv8, specifically the "nano" (YOLOv8n) variant, whose architectural lineage runs back to Redmon *et al.*'s (2016) original "You Only Look Once" formulation of object detection as a single-pass regression problem, rather than the slower two-stage detect-then-classify pipelines that preceded it. The specific YOLOv8 implementation used in this project is Ultralytics' own open-source release; because Ultralytics never published a formal peer-reviewed paper alongside the original YOLOv8 release, this draft — more carefully than the previous one — cites **both** the primary source and an independent secondary analysis, rather than treating the two as interchangeable. Jocher, Chaurasia and Qiu's (2023) `CITATION.cff`-registered software citation is the correct primary citation for the actual codebase this project imports (`ultralytics`, pinned in `requirements.txt`); Yaseen's (2024) independent architectural analysis is retained alongside it as the closest available substitute for the peer-reviewed methods paper Ultralytics itself never wrote, documenting the specific internal design choices (the anchor-free detection head, the C2f backbone module) that distinguish YOLOv8 from Redmon *et al.*'s 2016 original. This project's own validated 99.7% recall figure for YOLOv8n against ground-truth face annotations (documented in the Methodology chapter) sits comfortably within, and is directly comparable to, the general real-time-detection literature this lineage represents.

The proposal's originally named perception model, NVIDIA's LocateAnything-3B (Wang *et al.*, 2026), is a very recent open-vocabulary vision-language grounding model using parallel box decoding rather than YOLO's grid-based regression, and this project's own direct, measured, head-to-head comparison against YOLOv8n — now extended well beyond the original offline comparison into a full live-simulation benchmark across 30 trials per policy per detector (`APPROACH_ACCURACY_RESULTS.md`) — is a genuine primary empirical contribution in its own right, not merely a documented substitution. The final live comparison is considerably more nuanced than the earlier offline-only comparison could show: at LocateAnything-3B's much lower achievable perception rate (roughly 0.5 Hz against YOLOv8n's 2 Hz), *median* approach accuracy is largely preserved or even slightly improved for two of the three policies, but the *character* of failure changes qualitatively — the rule-based baseline's group cut-through rate rises from 0 of 10 trials to 6 of 10 (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.011; see Section 2.9), and all three policies occasionally produce a catastrophic outlier trial (2–3.5 m from any valid target) that never occurs under the faster detector. No published paper this review could locate has previously reported a directly measured speed/accuracy/robustness comparison between a general-purpose vision-language grounding model and a specialised real-time detector on cluttered, robot-mounted-camera indoor human-robot-interaction footage, which this project's Results and Discussion chapter is positioned to report as an original empirical finding.

Finally, the navigation and platform layers this project runs on both have citable, primary-source system documentation rather than only vendor pages, and both are cited again in the Methodology and Implementation chapters when TIAGo and Nav2 are first introduced as the experimental platform: Macenski *et al.*'s (2020) "Marathon 2" paper is the actual system paper behind ROS 2's Nav2 stack, and Pagès, Marchionni and Ferro's (2016) paper introduces the TIAGo platform itself, presented at the International Workshop on Robot Modularity co-located with IROS 2016 in Daejeon (see Section 2.8).

### 2.8 Platform and Navigation Stack

This short section exists to give the platform this project's entire live evaluation depends on its own proper, separate treatment rather than folding it into the perception section as an afterthought. Macenski *et al.*'s (2020) "Marathon 2" paper describes the design of Nav2, the ROS 2 navigation framework this project's rule-based and learned policies alike issue `NavigateToPose` goals to, and its layered-costmap architecture (itself descended from Lu, Hershberger and Smart, 2014, Section 2.2) is the actual mechanism by which social-navigation-style costs can be incorporated into planning at all, even though this project's own social behaviour is implemented as an outer policy layer choosing *where to send the goal* rather than as a custom Nav2 costmap plugin. Pagès, Marchionni and Ferro's (2016) paper introduces TIAGo, the PAL Robotics mobile-manipulator platform this project's entire simulated system is built around, presented at the International Workshop on Robot Modularity, co-located with the IEEE/RSJ International Conference on Intelligent Robots and Systems (IROS) 2016 in Daejeon, South Korea, on 10 October 2016 — venue details now confirmed directly against the workshop's own archived programme page rather than inferred, resolving the uncertainty flagged in the previous draft of this review.

**Limitation of this literature relative to the project:** both papers are, honestly, platform and system-architecture descriptions rather than social-navigation research contributions in their own right — they are cited because citing the actual software and hardware a project's experimental results depend on, rather than only a marketing page, is standard and expected academic practice, not because either paper makes a claim this project's own argument rests on.

### 2.9 Experimental Methodology and Statistical Treatment for Small-Sample HRI Evaluation

This section is new in this draft, and its absence previously was arguably the most consequential gap in the whole review, because this project's finished live evaluation is a genuinely small-sample experiment — ten trials per policy per detector condition, twelve conditions in total — and a Masters-level dissertation reporting such a study needs its statistical choices grounded in the methodology literature, not merely applied silently in a results script. Hoffman and Zhao's (2020) "A Primer for Conducting Experiments in Human–Robot Interaction" is the field's own standard methodological reference for exactly this situation: designing, running, and honestly reporting a controlled HRI experiment, including explicit guidance on statistical power, the dangers of overinterpreting small-sample comparisons, and the importance of reporting effect sizes and raw counts rather than only significance labels when a sample is small. This project's own evaluation script (`summarise_sim_results.py`) and its documentation (`Section_3_6_Evaluation_Metrics.md`, §3.6.4) follow this guidance closely and, on reflection, independently: binary outcomes are compared with the two-sided Fisher exact test rather than a chi-squared approximation inappropriate at this sample size, raw trial counts ("8/10") are reported alongside percentages specifically so that a small number of trials cannot present itself as more statistically solid than it is, and — most tellingly — the project's own documentation states explicitly that "at n = 10 per condition the study is powered to detect large effects only. Where a difference is substantial but does not reach p < 0.05, this is stated explicitly and the effect size is reported rather than the difference being described as significant." This is precisely the discipline Hoffman and Zhao's primer argues for, and citing it here converts what might otherwise read as an ad-hoc statistical choice into a methodologically grounded one.

Fisher's (1922) original paper, "On the Interpretation of χ² from Contingency Tables, and the Calculation of P," is the primary source for the exact test this project's own statistical comparisons actually use — the two-sided Fisher exact test that produces the project's own headline p = 0.011 result for the rule-based baseline's group-cut-through-rate increase under the slower detector (Section 2.7). It is included here on the same principle as Breiman (2001) and Friedman (2001) in Section 2.6: a dissertation that reports a p-value owes that test its own primary citation, not merely a mention that "a statistical test was used."

**Limitation of this literature relative to the project:** Hoffman and Zhao's primer is written for experiments involving real human participants, and this project's evaluation is conducted entirely in simulation against scripted actor models — a difference the primer itself would regard as significant, since much of its guidance (participant recruitment, counterbalancing, demand characteristics, subjective questionnaires) does not transfer to a simulation study at all. What does transfer, and what this project draws on specifically, is the primer's guidance on statistical power and honest reporting at small sample sizes, which applies regardless of whether the "participants" are human or simulated.

**Closing gap statement.** Taken together, the reviewed literature establishes a rigorous theoretical vocabulary for human spatial and social behaviour (Hall, 1966; Kendon, 1990); general principles of human-aware robot navigation that explicitly name group-specific approach as an under-addressed sub-problem (Kruse *et al.*, 2013; Ríos-Martínez, Spalanzani and Laugier, 2015; Mavrogiannis *et al.*, 2023; Singamaneni *et al.*, 2024); increasingly sophisticated computational tools for detecting group structure, both hand-designed and learned, some of which do not depend on the orientation estimates this project's own dataset cannot supply (Cristani *et al.*, 2011; Setti *et al.*, 2015; Vascon *et al.*, 2016; Swofford *et al.*, 2020); a small robotics literature that hand-designs approach behaviour for single people or dynamically-tracked groups, or supplies a cleanly annotated group-approach dataset distinct from this project's own harder corpus (Satake *et al.*, 2009; Truong and Ngo, 2017; Repiso, Garrell and Sanfeliu, 2020; Yang *et al.*, 2021); a mature and increasingly self-critical Learning from Demonstration literature that supplies both the Behavioural Cloning paradigm this project uses and an honest, well-documented account of exactly why and how it fails under limited or poorly-covering demonstration data (Argall *et al.*, 2009; Ravichandar *et al.*, 2020; Pomerleau, 1989; Bojarski *et al.*, 2016; Ross, Gordon and Bagnell, 2011; Torabi, Warnell and Stone, 2018; Codevilla *et al.*, 2019); properly-grounded supervised-learning algorithms whose independent convergence to the same performance ceiling is itself informative (Breiman, 2001; Friedman, 2001; Ke *et al.*, 2017); a supporting perception and platform literature this project directly measures against and builds upon (Redmon *et al.*, 2016; Jocher, Chaurasia and Qiu, 2023; Yaseen, 2024; Wang *et al.*, 2026; Macenski *et al.*, 2020; Pagès, Marchionni and Ferro, 2016); and a methodological literature for conducting and honestly reporting small-sample HRI experiments (Hoffman and Zhao, 2020; Fisher, 1922).

**No existing work combines all of the following in one evaluated system:** person perception validated against ground truth on a genuinely non-expert, retrospectively-repurposed dataset; group and O-space estimation derived under a documented, literature-justified position-only approximation; a Behavioural Cloning policy trained on that data and evaluated not only offline but through a full live-simulation study against a hand-designed geometric rule; and — the most specific and most novel finding this literature review's framing sets up precisely — an explicit, measured **dissociation between position accuracy and orientation accuracy** as separately learnable and separately achievable properties of a group-approach policy, something none of the reviewed navigation surveys treats as a live possibility at all. This is the gap this dissertation addresses. Chapter 5 reports the result: across a 60-trial live simulation spanning two perception conditions, the tuned Behavioural Cloning policy beats the hand-designed rule decisively on position (a median error of 8.5 cm against the rule's 26.1 cm under the primary detector) while the rule beats every learned policy decisively on orientation (56.8–64.6° against the learned policies' 66.4–79.4°) — a split, honestly-reported result that neither confirms nor refutes the premise that learning improves on hand-designed rules, but instead sharpens it: learning improves on the part of the task where non-expert human demonstration data actually carries a strong, learnable signal, and does not improve on the part where a simple geometric constraint (face the centre) already does the job a demonstration-trained model cannot learn as reliably from a corpus this size.

### 2.11 Aims and Objectives Revisited

Situated within the literature reviewed above, this project's aim is to learn, via Behavioural Cloning trained on non-expert human demonstrations, a socially appropriate group-approach policy for a TIAGo robot — a specific sub-problem (Singamaneni *et al.*, 2024) that the wider human-aware navigation literature (Section 2.2) has left comparatively under-addressed relative to obstacle avoidance and individual-pedestrian interaction, grounded throughout in Kendon's (1990) and Hall's (1966) theoretical account of what a socially appropriate group approach actually consists of (Section 2.1), and evaluated against the existing group-approach robotics literature's exclusively hand-designed policies (Section 2.4) using a demonstration dataset considerably less clean than the closest published analogue (Yang *et al.*, 2021).

The project's four supporting objectives each map onto a specific gap identified above, and can now be restated against the project's actually-completed results rather than only its planned methodology. Objective 2 (person localisation) addresses the F-formation-detection literature's own dependency on reliable position estimates (Section 2.3) and was validated at 99.7% recall against real ground truth rather than assumed. Objective 3 (group/O-space detection) adopts a position-only fallback justified by, rather than merely resembling, the dominant-set clustering literature's demonstrated independence from orientation data (Vascon *et al.*, 2016), and is now complete, having been validated against thirty hand-labelled frames. Objective 4 (Behavioural Cloning versus a rule-based baseline) directly tests the Learning-from-Demonstration literature's central open question — whether a learned policy can outperform a hand-designed one on a specific, well-defined task — using the diagnostic tools that same literature supplies (Ross, Gordon and Bagnell, 2011; Torabi, Warnell and Stone, 2018; Codevilla *et al.*, 2019) to explain *why* the answer turned out to be "yes, on position; no, on orientation," rather than reporting a single pass/fail verdict. And Objective 1, this literature review itself, is judged by the same standard the reviewed methodology literature (Hoffman and Zhao, 2020) sets for the rest of the dissertation: every claim traceable to a verified source, every algorithm and statistical test properly grounded rather than assumed self-evident, and every limitation stated as plainly as the corresponding strength.
