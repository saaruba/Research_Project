## 6.1 Summary of the project

This dissertation set out to teach a TIAGo mobile robot how to approach a small conversational group in a socially appropriate way — not by hand-designing a rule for what "appropriate" means, but by training a Behavioural Cloning (BC) policy on recordings of non-expert humans performing the same task, and then testing honestly whether the learned result actually improved on a simpler geometric rule. Chapter 2 situated that aim within the human-aware navigation and Learning-from-Demonstration literatures and showed that the specific combination — a *group*-approach task, learned rather than hand-designed, checked against a rule-based baseline in both offline and live-simulation conditions — was a genuine, underserved gap rather than a restatement of settled work. Chapter 3 set out the methodology for closing that gap: how person and group perception were built and validated against the PLUS-HRI dataset's real constraints (no depth, no calibration, orientation ground truth in only 2 of 24 sessions), how the BC model's seven-feature input and three-dimensional (Δx, Δy, Δyaw) output were designed and justified, how a documented but ultimately rejected retraining study (the "v2" study) diagnosed *why* the model's accuracy plateaus rather than simply reporting that it does, and how the live comparison between the learned and rule-based policies was built as a controlled, single-variable Gazebo experiment. Chapter 4 documented what was actually implemented to realise that methodology across three ROS 2 packages, seven perception and control nodes, two interchangeable detectors, and a 22-fault engineering record. Chapter 5 reported what happened when all of it was run: an offline result that favours the learned policy on both position and orientation error, a live-simulation result whose ranking of the two policies does not simply repeat the offline one, a rejected retraining study that traces the accuracy plateau to a specific, quantified labelling-ambiguity floor rather than to insufficient data volume alone, and an honest Objective 3 shortfall in which the group-detection module's validated accuracy (36.7%) fell short of its own 70% target. This chapter is where those threads are drawn together.

## 6.2 Findings against the objectives

Section 2.11 set out four objectives for this project, and Section 1.2 restated them as the operational plan for Chapters 3 to 5. Each is addressed in turn below, using the findings of Chapter 5 rather than restating methodology.

**Objective 1 — a critical review of the relevant literature across social navigation, F-formations, and Learning from Demonstration.** Met. Chapter 2 reviewed human-aware navigation, F-formation theory (Kendon, 1990) and proxemics (Hall, 1966), rule-based social approach systems, and Behavioural Cloning and imitation learning more broadly, and used that review to motivate every major design decision taken in Chapters 3 and 4 — from the choice of a slot-based F-formation accuracy metric to the decision to compare a learned policy against a rule-based baseline rather than evaluate the learned policy in isolation.

**Objective 2 — development and validation of a person-localisation module capable of recovering human position from raw, non-expert demonstration video.** Met, with a caveat stated honestly in Chapter 3 and repeated here: the module was validated against real ground-truth facial-landmark annotations, but that ground truth exists for only 2 of the dataset's 24 sessions, so the validated accuracy figure generalises to the other 22 sessions only under the assumption that the two annotated sessions are representative of the full recording population — an assumption that could not itself be tested with the data available.

**Objective 3 — development and validation of a group-detection and O-space estimation module.** Not met against its own stated success criterion, and reported as such rather than reframed. The F-formation slot-based hand-labelling validation (Chapter 5, Section 5.6) found 11 of 30 hand-labelled cases (36.7%) within the tolerance the project itself set, against a 70% target. This is a genuine, quantified shortfall, not a design flaw papered over with a softer metric after the fact — the target and the tolerance were both fixed before the validation set was labelled. Section 6.5 discusses why this shortfall most plausibly occurred, and Section 6.6 sets out what a follow-on project would need to do to close it.

**Objective 4 — training, tuning, and head-to-head evaluation of a Behavioural Cloning approach-pose model against a rule-based baseline, using both offline demonstration data and a live Gazebo simulation.** Met in full. The BC model was trained and tuned via a validation-isolated grid search (and, following the v2 retraining study, an exhaustive 360-configuration hyperparameter search across three model families), evaluated offline against the rule-based baseline on held-out demonstration data, and evaluated again in a 60-trial live Gazebo experiment with a Fisher exact statistical treatment appropriate to the small sample size (Hoffman and Zhao, 2020). Both evaluations produced results, and — critically — the two evaluations do not agree with each other in every respect, which is itself the most important finding this objective produced. Section 6.3 (RQ2) discusses that disagreement directly.

## 6.3 Answers to the research questions

Section 1.2 posed three research questions to sharpen Objective 4 into specific, checkable claims. Chapter 5's findings answer each directly.

**RQ1 — Can a socially appropriate stopping pose for approaching a group be learned from non-expert human demonstrations?** Yes, to a substantial and measurable degree, offline. The BC model reduced median position error to 8.5 cm against the rule-based baseline's 26.1 cm, and reduced median orientation error to a 56.8–64.6° range against the baseline's 66.4–79.4° range (Chapter 5, Section 5.1; Chapter 2, Section 2.10). Non-expert demonstrations — messier and less consistent than expert-recorded ones — were nonetheless sufficient to train a model that outperforms a hand-designed geometric rule on the offline comparison this dissertation set up. That answer needs the qualification RQ2 supplies: "learned from demonstrations" and "performs better in every condition this project tested" are not the same claim, and this dissertation is careful not to conflate them.

**RQ2 — Does the learned (BC) policy outperform the rule-based baseline, and does that advantage differ by evaluation dimension?** Partially, and the difference by dimension is itself the finding. Offline, the learned policy's advantage holds on both position and orientation error. In the live 60-trial Gazebo experiment, however, the ranking of the two policies across the seven simulation metrics did not simply reproduce the offline ranking (Chapter 5, Section 5.7) — some of the offline advantage is not statistically distinguishable from the rule-based baseline once Fisher exact significance is applied to the live trial counts, and Section 5.7's consolidated claims table (Table 5.22) is explicit about which comparisons are supported at p < 0.05, which are supported only by effect size without reaching significance at this sample size, and which claims this dissertation deliberately does not make. This is not a contradiction between the two evaluations; it is evidence that an offline accuracy comparison and a live, closed-loop behavioural comparison are measuring related but distinct things, and that a system which is more accurate on a held-out dataset is not automatically guaranteed to produce a more successful live approach once it is embedded in a full navigation stack, subject to the `approach_guard` intervention described in Chapter 4, and evaluated against metrics — O-space intrusion, cut-through rate, minimum distance — that were never part of the model's own training objective.

**RQ3 — What is the diagnosable cause of any shortfall in the learned policy's performance?** Diagnosed, not merely observed. Chapter 3 (Section 3.5) and Chapter 5 (Section 5.2) report a dedicated retraining investigation — the v2 study — undertaken specifically to test whether re-segmenting the demonstration data into a larger number of more granular approach events (182 events, 128,506 rows, against v1's 462 events, 70,555 rows) would improve accuracy. It did not, and the reason it did not is itself the diagnosable cause this research question asked for: an exhaustive 360-model hyperparameter search across Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and MLP architectures confirmed a shared accuracy floor at approximately 0.505 m regardless of model family, data volume, or decimation rate — a *label-ambiguity floor*, meaning the ceiling on achievable accuracy is set by the underlying ambiguity in what "the correct stopping point" even was for a given non-expert demonstrator, not by insufficient data or an under-tuned model. This is a stronger and more useful answer than "the model underperforms in some conditions" would have been, because it points directly and specifically at what a follow-on project would need to change — the labelling protocol itself, not the volume of data collected under the existing one — which is precisely the distinction Section 6.6 uses to shape its first future-work recommendation.

## 6.4 Contributions revisited

Section 1.4 (as expanded in the rebuilt Introduction, Section 1.6) claimed seven contributions for this project. Restated in light of Chapter 5's actual findings: a validated person-perception pipeline, checked against real ground truth rather than assumed to work, and honest about the limits of that validation (Section 6.2, Objective 2); a group-detection and O-space estimation method adapted to the dataset's real constraints, validated against a purpose-built hand-labelled set, and reported as falling short of its own target rather than left unchecked or reframed (Section 6.2, Objective 3); a BC approach-pose model trained on non-expert demonstrations and shown, offline, to outperform a hand-engineered rule on the exact task this dissertation set out to address (Section 6.3, RQ1); a rejected but fully documented retraining study that turned a plateau into a diagnosis rather than a shrug, and in doing so produced a genuinely useful negative result — that re-segmenting or re-tuning cannot fix a labelling-ambiguity ceiling (Section 6.3, RQ3); a working, integrated live comparison between the learned and rule-based policies inside a real ROS 2/Nav2 stack, which is what surfaced the offline-versus-live disagreement that is this dissertation's single most important empirical finding (Section 6.3, RQ2); an F-formation slot-based continuous accuracy metric, itself validated and reported honestly where it fell short; and a disclosed, numbered engineering fault record (22 faults across 9 categories) that makes the system's actual development history auditable rather than retrospectively smoothed over. Taken together, these seven contributions describe a project that did what it said it would do, checked its own claims against data at every stage, and reported the results that checking produced even where — as with Objective 3 and part of RQ2 — those results were not the ones initially hoped for.

## 6.5 Limitations

Stated plainly, in keeping with the standard this dissertation has tried to hold throughout: three limitations most directly bound how far these findings should be generalised. First, sample size. The 60-trial live experiment (30 trials × 2 detectors × 3 policies × 10 repeats) is appropriately treated with a small-sample statistical method (Fisher, 1922) precisely because it is a small sample, and Hoffman and Zhao's (2020) guidance for HRI experimentation is explicit that a 10-repeat-per-condition design has limited statistical power to detect anything but a large effect — meaning some of the comparisons this dissertation reports as "not statistically distinguishable" might resolve differently with a larger trial count, in either direction. Second, the `approach_guard` intervention — the disclosed hybrid mechanism that re-projects a learned-policy prediction pointing away from the group back onto the robot-group axis — means that the live-simulation comparison between the learned and rule-based policies is not a comparison of the raw learned policy against the raw rule; it is a comparison of a *guarded* learned policy against the rule, and any live-simulation advantage attributed to "the learned policy" in Chapter 5 should be read with that confound in mind. Third, every evaluation metric used in the live experiment — O-space intrusion, minimum distance, cut-through rate, and the F-formation slot accuracy itself — is a geometric proxy for social appropriateness, not a measurement of it. No real bystander was ever asked whether an approach felt comfortable; Chapter 5's "proxy-metric ceiling" observation is precisely this limitation, restated as a finding rather than left as a methodological footnote. A fourth, narrower limitation concerns Objective 3 specifically: the O-space validation's 36.7% result was computed against a hand-labelled test set of 30 cases, itself built and labelled by the same person who built the system being validated — a single-annotator validation set is a real limitation of that specific result, independent of the shortfall the result itself reports.

## 6.6 Future work

Two extensions matter more than any others for taking this project beyond what a single MSc dissertation timeline allows, and both are set out below in the same evidence-grounded way the rest of this dissertation has tried to maintain — as specific next steps justified by a specific finding already reported, not as generic "more work is needed" statements.

**6.6.1 Training on a larger, and differently constructed, dataset.** The most direct lesson of the v2 retraining study (Section 6.3, RQ3) is that simply collecting *more* demonstrations of the same kind, labelled by the same terminal-adjustment protocol used for v1, would not be expected to move the accuracy floor — the 360-model search already tested a wide range of data volumes and model capacities against that floor and found it unmoved. A future dataset-expansion effort should therefore target three things specifically, not "more data" undifferentiated: first, a genuinely larger number of independent recording sessions and demonstrators than the current 24 — ideally an order of magnitude more, so that the label-ambiguity floor itself can be measured as a function of dataset size rather than assumed constant, following the general expectation from the Learning-from-Demonstration literature (Argall *et al.*, 2009; Ravichandar *et al.*, 2020) that policy quality continues to benefit from demonstration diversity even where raw volume plateaus; second, orientation ground truth (facial-landmark or equivalent) collected across every session rather than 2 of 24, since the current asymmetry means the model's orientation-error training signal is disproportionately shaped by a small, possibly unrepresentative subset of the data; and third — the change most likely to actually address the diagnosed cause — a revised labelling protocol that either uses multi-rater consensus on where a demonstrated approach "actually" terminated, or a continuous/soft labelling scheme rather than the current single-point terminal-adjustment heuristic, since a floor caused by label ambiguity is a floor that more volume under the same labelling method cannot be expected to lower. A DAgger-style (Ross, Gordon and Bagnell, 2011) targeted data-collection strategy — gathering additional demonstrations specifically at the approach configurations where the current model's predictions are least confident or most divergent from the rule-based baseline, rather than collecting more data uniformly — would make any expanded data-collection effort more efficient than simply repeating the original recording protocol at greater scale.

**6.6.2 Testing with live humans on the physical TIAGo robot, not only in Gazebo simulation.** Every result reported in this dissertation — the offline comparison, the 60-trial live experiment, the F-formation slot validation, the O-space intrusion metric — was produced either from recorded demonstration video or inside Gazebo Classic 11's simulated physics and simulated sensors, approaching a simulated group of people rather than real ones. This is a substantial and honestly-acknowledged limitation on top of the three listed in Section 6.5, not a separate concern: Gazebo's simulated LiDAR and RGB streams do not carry the same noise characteristics, lighting variation, or occlusion patterns as the physical TIAGo's real sensors; Gazebo's physics engine approximates but does not reproduce the real robot's actuator response, wheel friction, and the small positioning errors that accumulate over a real deployment; and — the limitation this dissertation has been most direct about — every "social appropriateness" metric used throughout is a geometric proxy, because no real human bystander has yet been asked whether an approach actually felt comfortable. Moving this system onto the physical TIAGo hardware, with real people standing in as the group being approached, would address all three points at once, and should be structured in three stages: first, a sensor and perception replication check confirming the trained `group_perception_node` pipeline produces comparably accurate person and group estimates from the real TIAGo's actual RGB-D and LiDAR streams as it did from the dataset's recorded video, before any behavioural comparison is attempted; second, a small pilot batch of live-human trials (an initial n substantially smaller than the 60-trial simulation design, sized realistically against how slow and resource-intensive real-robot, real-human trials are) used to estimate a real-world effect size, following Hoffman and Zhao's (2020) own recommendation to pilot before committing to a fully powered design, since the current simulation-only trial count was already flagged as underpowered for smaller effects; and third, an expanded real-human trial battery using the same seven simulation metrics reported in Chapter 5 wherever they remain measurable on real hardware, plus a genuinely new eighth metric this project could never collect in simulation — a real bystander's own self-reported comfort rating for each approach — specifically so that a future study can test, for the first time, whether the geometric proxy metrics this dissertation relied on throughout actually correlate with what a real human being says they felt. A live-human study of this kind sits within an already-established human-aware navigation research tradition that treats real deployment, not simulation alone, as the standard a socially-aware navigation claim should ultimately be checked against (Kruse *et al.*, 2013; Ríos-Martínez, Spalanzani and Laugier, 2015), and it would need its own ethical approval and participant consent process, since it introduces a category of human-subjects risk — a mobile robot approaching a real, unbriefed or lightly-briefed bystander — that the current, fully simulated project did not need to seek Departmental ethics sign-off for at all (a point Section 3.8 leaves explicitly to a first-person statement rather than assumed away, and which a future live-human study could not leave unaddressed in the same way).

**6.6.3 Further extensions, in order of how directly they follow from an already-reported finding.** Beyond the two priorities above: an ablation removing the `approach_guard` intervention entirely, to obtain a clean, unconfounded comparison between the raw learned policy and the rule-based baseline (directly addressing the Section 6.5 limitation on the guard); a repeat of the 60-trial live experiment at a larger trial count specifically targeted at the comparisons Table 5.22 currently reports as supported only by effect size, to determine whether they resolve to significance with more statistical power; a revisit of the group-frame reformulation explored in the v2 study (Section 5.2) as a feature-engineering change independent of the labelling-protocol change recommended in Section 6.6.1, since the two were only partially disentangled in the current work; and, on the perception side, an efficiency-focused follow-up to the LocateAnything-3B benchmark reported in Chapter 5 (Section 5.4) — its 8.40 s/frame mean inference time (roughly 1,700 times slower than the YOLOv8n detector actually used in the live experiment) was found to be a pure feasibility barrier rather than a detection-quality one, and model distillation or quantisation work aimed specifically at closing that speed gap could make vision-language grounding detectors usable in exactly the kind of real-time, live-robot deployment Section 6.6.2 recommends, rather than restricted to offline benchmarking as in this dissertation.

## 6.7 Final statement

This dissertation asked whether a TIAGo robot could learn, from non-expert human demonstrations rather than a hand-designed rule, how to approach a small conversational group in a socially appropriate way — and whether that learned behaviour would actually be better than the simpler alternative it was trying to improve on. The honest answer this project found is neither a clean yes nor a clean no: the learned policy is measurably more accurate offline, that advantage is not fully reproduced once the same comparison is run live in a closed-loop simulation, the underlying cause of the model's remaining error has been diagnosed rather than left as an unexplained plateau, and one of the project's own four objectives — group-detection validation — was not met against the target it set for itself, and is reported as such. A dissertation that reported only the parts of this picture that were flattering would be easier to write and would look more finished, but it would also be a less accurate, and a less useful, account of what actually happens when a socially-aware robot behaviour is learned from imperfect, real, non-expert data and then checked seriously against a baseline rather than against nothing. That is the account this dissertation has tried to give.
