# Chapter 1

# Introduction

Picture a service robot working the floor of a busy hotel lobby, a museum atrium, or a hospital waiting area. Most of the research literature on robot navigation has spent the last two decades teaching that robot one specific skill extremely well: how to get from A to B without hitting anything, and without startling anyone on the way (Kruse *et al.*, 2013; Ríos-Martínez, Spalanzani and Laugier, 2015). That skill is necessary, but it is not the skill this scenario actually calls for. The robot in this lobby does not need to avoid the small cluster of three people stood talking near the reception desk — it needs to *approach* them, stop at a distance that reads as helpful rather than intrusive, and turn to face the conversation rather than one person's shoulder. Deciding where to stop, and which way to turn, is a completely different problem from deciding how to get there without a collision, and it is a problem the human-aware navigation literature has only recently started to name explicitly as a distinct, underserved challenge in its own right (Mavrogiannis *et al.*, 2023; Singamaneni *et al.*, 2024). This dissertation is about teaching a robot that second skill — not by writing a rule for it, but by showing it examples of how non-expert humans already do it, and asking whether the robot can learn to do the same.

## 1.1 Motivation and problem statement

Human beings solve this problem so effortlessly that it barely registers as a problem at all. Decades of research in social psychology, beginning with Kendon's (1990) study of *F-formations* — the stable spatial arrangements small groups fall into when they interact — shows that conversation naturally produces a shared, jointly "owned" space at its centre, ringed by the space the participants themselves occupy, and an outer zone from which a newcomer can be seen approaching before they arrive. Joining that conversation gracefully means approaching from a direction the group can see coming, choosing a gap in the formation rather than barging through its middle, and stopping at a distance and orientation that signals "I'd like to join" without signalling "I am about to sit on your lap." People do all of this without conscious calculation. A robot has none of that intuition for free — it has to be given it, either by a human engineer writing down an explicit rule for what "a good approach" looks like, or by a learning system inferring the rule from examples of humans actually doing it.

Both routes have been tried in the literature, but rarely both at once, and rarely on the specific sub-problem of *approaching a group* rather than a single person. Rule-based approaches (Satake *et al.*, 2009; Truong and Ngo, 2017; Repiso, Garrell and Sanfeliu, 2019) demonstrate convincingly that approach direction and distance measurably affect how comfortable an interaction feels, but the rule itself is hand-designed by the researcher, encoding one particular guess at what "socially appropriate" means rather than deriving it from observed human behaviour. Learning-based approaches to robot behaviour more broadly are well established (Argall *et al.*, 2009; Ravichandar *et al.*, 2020), and Behavioural Cloning specifically — training a policy to imitate recorded demonstrations directly, an idea dating back to Pomerleau's (1989) original self-driving neural network — offers a route to a policy that reflects what real (if non-expert) humans actually chose to do, rather than what a designer assumed they would want. What is missing is a system that combines the two: perceiving a real conversational group in a real environment, estimating its social geometry the way F-formation theory describes it, and then *learning*, rather than hand-designing, how to approach it — and then, critically, checking honestly whether the learned version is actually any better than the simple geometric rule it is trying to improve on. That combination, and that honest comparison, is the specific gap this dissertation addresses, and Chapter 2 situates it precisely within the wider literature once the full picture of related work has been laid out.

## 1.2 Aims and objectives

The aim of this project is to develop and evaluate a system in which a TIAGo mobile robot learns, via Behavioural Cloning trained on non-expert human demonstrations, to approach small conversational groups in a socially appropriate way, and to determine whether that learned behaviour outperforms a simpler, hand-engineered geometric rule on the same task. Four objectives operationalise that aim, each addressed in detail in the chapters that follow: a critical review of the relevant literature across social navigation, F-formations, and Learning from Demonstration (Chapter 2); the development and validation of a person-localisation module capable of recovering human position from raw, non-expert demonstration video (Chapters 3–4); the development and validation of a group-detection and O-space estimation module built on that perception output (Chapters 3–4); and the training, tuning, and head-to-head evaluation of a Behavioural Cloning approach-pose model against a rule-based baseline, using both offline demonstration data and a live Gazebo simulation (Chapters 3–5). Section 2.1 revisits these objectives in full, alongside the specific, measurable success criteria attached to each, once the literature that motivates those criteria has been reviewed.

## 1.3 Why this problem, and why this dataset

Two practical decisions shape everything that follows in this dissertation, and both are worth stating plainly here rather than leaving them to be inferred later. First, this project deliberately learns from *non-expert* demonstrations rather than curated, expert-recorded ones. A robot destined for a real hotel lobby or hospital waiting room will ultimately need to behave well for the humans who actually populate those spaces, not for a small number of HRI researchers who have rehearsed the "correct" way to approach a stranger — non-expert demonstrations are messier, more varied, and arguably a more honest signal of what a broad population of people actually consider a natural approach, even though (as this dissertation will show) they are also considerably harder to learn from. Second, this project works with the PLUS-HRI dataset: 24 recorded teleoperation sessions that were not collected with this specific research question in mind, and which — as direct inspection early in this project revealed — turn out to split into two quite different recording generations, only two of which carry the kind of face-level annotation originally expected to support group and orientation estimation. Rather than treating that as a reason to abandon the dataset, this project treats it as the more realistic starting condition: a robot deployed in the real world will never be handed a perfectly annotated, depth-calibrated feed of the people around it either, and a perception and learning pipeline that can extract social structure from an imperfect, retrospectively-repurposed dataset is arguably a more useful thing to have built than one that only works given data collected specifically to make the problem easy.

## 1.4 Contributions

This dissertation makes four contributions, each corresponding to one of the objectives above and reported in full in the chapters that follow. It contributes a validated person-perception pipeline, built and checked against real ground-truth annotations rather than assumed to work, that recovers human position from a dataset never designed to make that easy. It contributes a group-detection and O-space estimation method adapted specifically to that dataset's real constraints — no depth, no calibration, and no orientation ground truth at all — validated against a purpose-built, hand-labelled test set rather than left unchecked. It contributes a Behavioural Cloning model trained to predict a socially appropriate approach pose directly from non-expert demonstrations, tuned rigorously via a validation-only grid search rather than hand-picked defaults, and evaluated honestly — including reporting where it does *not* beat the simpler alternative, which is itself a genuine and useful finding rather than a disappointing one to bury. And it contributes a working, integrated robot system — perception, group estimation, and either policy, running live through Nav2 in a simulated environment — built specifically so that the comparison between the learned and rule-based approaches is a controlled, single-variable experiment rather than an offline number on its own. Chapter 5 reports what these contributions, taken together, actually found.

## 1.5 Alignment with the MSc Computer Science programme

This project sits within robotics and machine learning, two of the core research areas within Computer Science, and specifically at their intersection: it applies supervised machine learning (Behavioural Cloning, feature engineering, model selection, and hyperparameter tuning via a properly validation-isolated grid search) to a robotics perception-and-control problem (person detection, spatial clustering, and navigation via a modern ROS 2/Nav2 stack), evaluated with the rigour a Masters-level research project in Computer Science is expected to bring: session-level data splitting to avoid leakage, held-out test evaluation performed only once, and a stated, honest accounting of where a chosen method's own limitations — dataset size, feature design, distributional shift — most likely explain its results, rather than treating an unfavourable result as a failure to be minimised. The project also draws directly on human-computer interaction and social-robotics research (F-formation theory, proxemics, human-aware navigation) to define what "success" should even mean for a robotics system whose ultimate audience is a human being rather than a benchmark score — a framing this dissertation treats as central rather than incidental, since a technically accurate approach pose that a real bystander would find uncomfortable is not, in any meaningful sense, a successful one.

## 1.6 Dissertation structure

The remainder of this dissertation is organised as follows. **Chapter 2 (Literature Review)** situates this project within the wider research on human-aware and socially aware robot navigation, F-formations and group-approach behaviour specifically, and Learning from Demonstration and Behavioural Cloning, closing with an explicit statement of the gap this dissertation addresses and a revisitation of the aims and objectives introduced above. **Chapter 3 (Methodology)** sets out the project's overall research design and the reasoning behind every major methodological choice — why a quantitative comparative experiment rather than a qualitative study, how the dataset's own limitations shaped the perception, clustering, and validation approach, how the Behavioural Cloning model's target and features were designed and why, and how the simulation environment and evaluation metrics were constructed to make the rule-vs-learned comparison a fair, single-variable one. **Chapter 4 (Implementation)** describes what was actually built to realise that methodology: the software architecture, toolchain, and engineering choices behind the three-package ROS 2 system, the concrete algorithms behind clustering, approach-point selection, and the rule-based policy's staged refinement, the models' technical implementation, and how the system was tested and validated. **Chapter 5 (Results & Discussion)** reports what happened when the resulting system was run — both the offline comparison between the learned and rule-based policies against the demonstrated dataset, and the live simulation results — and discusses those findings critically against the aims and objectives set out here. **Chapter 6 (Conclusion)** summarises what this project achieved, states its limitations plainly, and sets out what a continuation of this work should address next.
