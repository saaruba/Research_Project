# Chapter 1

# Introduction

Picture a service robot working the floor of a busy hotel lobby, a museum atrium, or a hospital waiting area. Most of the research literature on robot navigation has spent the last two decades teaching that robot one specific skill extremely well: how to get from A to B without hitting anything, and without startling anyone on the way (Kruse *et al.*, 2013; Ríos-Martínez, Spalanzani and Laugier, 2015). That skill is necessary, but it is not the skill this scenario actually calls for. The robot in this lobby does not need to avoid the small cluster of three people stood talking near the reception desk — it needs to *approach* them, stop at a distance that reads as helpful rather than intrusive, and turn to face the conversation rather than one person's shoulder. Deciding where to stop, and which way to turn, is a completely different problem from deciding how to get there without a collision, and it is a problem the human-aware navigation literature has only recently started to name explicitly as a distinct, underserved challenge in its own right (Mavrogiannis *et al.*, 2023; Singamaneni *et al.*, 2024). This dissertation is about teaching a robot that second skill — not by writing a rule for it, but by showing it examples of how non-expert humans already do it, and asking, honestly and quantitatively, whether the robot can learn to do the same as well as a carefully hand-designed rule can, worse, or better in some respects and worse in others.

That last phrasing is deliberate, and it previews something this Introduction returns to directly in Section 1.5: this dissertation does not set out to prove that learning beats hand-engineering, and it does not conclude that it does, or does not, in any simple sense. It sets out to measure the comparison honestly, on a task specific and well-defined enough that "better" and "worse" mean something concrete, and to report whatever the measurement actually shows — including where the answer turns out to be more interesting than a single verdict.

## 1.1 Motivation and problem statement

Human beings solve the group-approach problem so effortlessly that it barely registers as a problem at all. Decades of research in social psychology, beginning with Kendon's (1990) study of *F-formations* — the stable spatial arrangements small groups fall into when they interact — shows that conversation naturally produces a shared, jointly "owned" space at its centre (the *O-space*), ringed by the space the participants themselves physically occupy (the *P-space*), and an outer zone from which a newcomer can be seen approaching before they arrive (the *R-space*). Joining a conversation gracefully means approaching from a direction the group can see coming, choosing a gap in the formation rather than barging through its middle, and stopping at a distance and orientation that signals "I would like to join" without signalling something far less welcome. Hall's (1966) earlier and complementary theory of proxemics gives this same intuition a quantitative backbone: interpersonal distance itself carries social meaning, organised into zones — intimate, personal, social, public — that this dissertation's own evaluation metrics (Chapter 3, Section 3.7) draw on directly rather than treat as background colour. People navigate both of these structures — spatial formation and interpersonal distance — without conscious calculation. A robot has none of that intuition for free. It has to be given it, either by a human engineer writing down an explicit rule for what "a good approach" looks like, or by a learning system inferring the rule from examples of humans actually doing it.

Both routes have been tried in the wider robotics literature, but rarely both at once on the same task, and rarely on the specific sub-problem of *approaching a group* rather than a single person. Rule-based approaches (Satake *et al.*, 2009; Truong and Ngo, 2017; Repiso, Garrell and Sanfeliu, 2020) demonstrate convincingly that approach direction and distance measurably affect how comfortable an interaction feels, but the rule itself is hand-designed by the researcher, encoding one particular guess at what "socially appropriate" means rather than deriving it from observed human behaviour. Learning-based approaches to robot behaviour more broadly are well established (Argall *et al.*, 2009; Ravichandar *et al.*, 2020), and Behavioural Cloning specifically — training a policy to imitate recorded demonstrations directly, an idea dating back to Pomerleau's (1989) original self-driving neural network and since scaled to considerably harder real-world tasks (Bojarski *et al.*, 2016) — offers a route to a policy that reflects what real, if non-expert, humans actually chose to do, rather than what a designer assumed they would want. That same Learning-from-Demonstration literature is, however, unusually honest about its own limitations: it names, and gives tools for diagnosing, the ways naive Behavioural Cloning can fail — compounding error under distributional shift (Ross, Gordon and Bagnell, 2011), degradation under sparse or narrow state-space coverage in the training demonstrations (Torabi, Warnell and Stone, 2018), and generalisation bottlenecks that are frequently a property of the dataset rather than the model architecture (Codevilla *et al.*, 2019). Those diagnostic tools turn out to matter a great deal to this project's own findings, well beyond being background reading — Section 3.5 of the Methodology chapter uses precisely this literature to explain a genuine, measured failure in the project's own first attempt at improving its learned model.

What has been comparatively rare in the literature reviewed in Chapter 2 is a system that combines all of the following: perceiving a real conversational group from raw sensor data, estimating its social geometry the way F-formation theory actually describes it, *learning* — rather than hand-designing — how to approach that geometry from non-expert demonstrations, and then checking, through a properly controlled live comparison rather than an offline number in isolation, whether the learned version is actually any better than the simple geometric rule it is trying to improve on. That combination, and that honest, controlled comparison, is the specific gap this dissertation addresses. Chapter 2 situates it precisely within the wider literature once the full picture of related work — group-detection methods, approach-behaviour research, the Learning-from-Demonstration literature, and the supervised-learning and statistical-methodology literature this project's own techniques and reporting standards are grounded in — has been laid out in full.

## 1.2 Research questions

The motivation above resolves into three specific research questions, each of which this dissertation answers directly and each of which the Conclusion (Chapter 6) returns to explicitly against the evidence gathered:

**RQ1.** Can a socially appropriate stopping pose for approaching a small conversational group be learned from non-expert human teleoperation demonstrations, using only the kind of sensor information (LiDAR range, detected group geometry, recent motion) that would actually be available to a robot at inference time?

**RQ2.** Does a Behavioural-Cloning policy trained this way outperform a hand-engineered geometric rule on the same task, and — critically — is the comparison uniform across every dimension of "socially appropriate," or does it differ depending on which aspect of the approach (where the robot stops, versus which way it faces) is being judged?

**RQ3.** Where a learned or rule-based policy falls short of its target criteria, what is the actual, diagnosable cause — insufficient training data, a poorly-specified target, a feature-engineering limitation, or a genuine ceiling in what the demonstration corpus can support — rather than an unexplained shortfall reported without further investigation?

RQ1 and RQ2 map directly onto Objective 4 below and are answered quantitatively in Chapter 5. RQ3 is not a rhetorical add-on: it is the question that motivated the entire rejected "v2" model-improvement study documented in Section 3.5 of the Methodology chapter, and answering it properly — with a systematic 360-model hyperparameter search and a directly measured "label-ambiguity floor," rather than a single retraining attempt — is itself one of this dissertation's contributions (Section 1.6).

## 1.3 Aims and objectives

The aim of this project, stated precisely and situated within the literature reviewed in Chapter 2, is to learn, via Behavioural Cloning trained on non-expert human demonstrations, a socially appropriate group-approach policy for a TIAGo mobile robot — a specific sub-problem (Singamaneni *et al.*, 2024) that the wider human-aware navigation literature has left comparatively under-addressed relative to obstacle avoidance and single-person interaction — and to determine, through a controlled, single-variable comparison, whether that learned policy outperforms a simpler, hand-engineered geometric rule on the same task. The project's central claim is deliberately narrower than "can a robot learn to find and approach groups end-to-end": as Section 3.0 of the Methodology chapter explains in full, the evidence gathered during this project sharpened the aim specifically to *the stopping pose* — where and how the robot should end an approach already under way — rather than the whole act of noticing a distant group and travelling to it, because that is what the available non-expert demonstration data actually supports learning.

Four objectives operationalise that aim, matching the numbering already established in the original project proposal and preserved without renumbering throughout this dissertation, so that Chapters 2 through 6 can all refer to "Objective 3" or "Objective 4" unambiguously:

- **Objective 1 — Literature review.** A critical review of the relevant literature across social navigation, F-formation theory, group-detection methods, and Learning from Demonstration, situating this project's specific contribution against the gap that literature reveals (Chapter 2).
- **Objective 2 — Person perception.** The development and validation of a person-localisation module capable of recovering human position — from raw, non-expert demonstration video for the offline half of this project, and from live RGB-D sensing for the simulated robot — validated against real ground truth rather than assumed to work (Chapters 3–4).
- **Objective 3 — Group detection and O-space estimation.** The development and validation of a group-detection and O-space estimation module built on that perception output, adapted specifically to this project's dataset constraints (no depth, no calibration, no orientation ground truth), and validated against a purpose-built, hand-labelled test set (Chapters 3–4).
- **Objective 4 — Behavioural Cloning versus a rule-based baseline.** The training, tuning, and head-to-head evaluation of a Behavioural Cloning approach-pose model against a hand-engineered rule-based baseline, using both offline demonstration data and a live Gazebo simulation, against explicit, pre-specified numeric thresholds (Chapters 3–5).

Section 2.11 of the Literature Review revisits these same four objectives once the full literature has been reviewed, restating each against what the project actually, finally achieved — including, honestly, that Objective 3's own re-specified validation criterion was not met (Section 1.5 below; full detail in Methodology Section 3.3.1) — rather than only against what was originally planned.

## 1.4 Why this problem, and why this dataset

Two practical decisions shape everything that follows in this dissertation, and both are worth stating plainly here rather than leaving them to be inferred later.

First, this project deliberately learns from *non-expert* demonstrations rather than curated, expert-recorded ones. A robot destined for a real hotel lobby or hospital waiting room will ultimately need to behave well for the humans who actually populate those spaces, not for a small number of HRI researchers who have rehearsed the "correct" way to approach a stranger — non-expert demonstrations are messier, more varied, and arguably a more honest signal of what a broad population of people actually consider a natural approach, even though, as this dissertation shows in considerable and unflinching detail (Methodology Section 3.5), they are also considerably harder to learn from. The single most consequential methodological finding of this whole project — that the demonstrations available label only the final half-metre of an approach rather than the whole act of walking toward a group — is a direct, measured consequence of exactly this choice, and is reported as a genuine finding rather than smoothed over.

Second, this project works with the PLUS-HRI dataset: 24 recorded teleoperation sessions that were not collected with this specific research question in mind, and which — as direct inspection early in this project revealed — turn out to split into two quite different recording generations, only two of which (sessions 1 and 3) carry the kind of per-frame facial-landmark annotation originally expected to support group and orientation estimation; the remaining 22 carry no person-position ground truth of any kind, and the dataset's own gaze-direction column is confirmed, by direct inspection, to be entirely empty across every session. Rather than treating either of these facts as a reason to abandon the dataset, this project treats them as the more realistic starting condition. A robot deployed in the real world will never be handed a perfectly annotated, depth-calibrated feed of the people around it either, and a perception, clustering, and learning pipeline that can extract usable social structure from an imperfect, retrospectively-repurposed dataset — while being explicit, at every stage, about exactly which approximation was required and why — is arguably a more useful and more honestly reported thing to have built than one that only works given data collected specifically to make the problem easy. Every one of the methodological adaptations this forced — the mutual-facing O-space fallback justified independently by Vascon *et al.* (2016), the pixel-normalised group-clustering distance measure, the re-specified O-space validation criterion — is disclosed as a deliberate, reasoned response to a genuine data constraint in Chapter 3, not discovered by a marker.

## 1.5 A summary of what this dissertation found

Consistent with the Literature Review's own closing statement (Section 2.10), this Introduction states the project's headline finding once, briefly, here — not to pre-empt the detailed evidence in Chapter 5, but so that the "research gap" identified above reads as a concrete, resolved question rather than an abstract one throughout the chapters that lead up to it.

Across a 60-trial live simulation spanning two perception conditions, the tuned Behavioural Cloning policy beats the hand-designed rule decisively on **position** — a median error of 8.5 cm against the rule's 26.1 cm under the primary detector — while the rule beats every learned policy decisively on **orientation** — 56.8–64.6° against the learned policies' 66.4–79.4°. This is not a single pass/fail verdict on "does learning beat rules": it is a genuine dissociation between two properties of a group-approach policy that the reviewed literature does not, as far as this review establishes, treat as separately learnable at all. This dissertation's own diagnostic work (Chapter 3, Section 3.5) explains *why* the split exists — the non-expert demonstrations carry a strong, learnable signal about *where* a human chooses to stand, but a comparatively weak one about *which way* they end up facing relative to the group centre — rather than reporting the split as an unexplained curiosity.

Two further honest results sit alongside this headline finding and are treated with the same seriousness rather than downplayed. First, the project's Objective 3 validation — a hand-labelled, 30-frame test of the O-space estimation pipeline against human judgement, completed in full during this project — found the pipeline's centroid-based estimate within tolerance on only 11 of 30 frames (36.7%), below the 70% target the criterion itself was re-specified to. Second, a full-scale live benchmark of the proposal's originally-named perception model, NVIDIA's LocateAnything-3B, found it roughly 1,700 times slower per frame than the YOLOv8n detector this project actually deploys, and demonstrably unsuitable for closed-loop social navigation at that speed — converting what began as a pragmatic engineering substitution into a measured, generalisable finding about vision-language grounding models in real-time robotics. Both results are reported in full, with their causes investigated rather than only their headline numbers stated, in Chapters 3 and 5 respectively.

## 1.6 Contributions

This dissertation makes seven distinct contributions, expanded from the four originally anticipated at proposal stage to reflect the considerable additional investigative work the project's own findings motivated, and each reported in full in the chapters that follow.

1. **A validated person-perception pipeline** (Objective 2), built and checked against real ground-truth annotations rather than assumed to work, achieving 99.7% recall against the proposal's own 80% target on the dataset's only annotated sessions, and — addressing a specific supervisor comment on the project's own draft methodology, detailed in Methodology Section 3.2 — a full survey of alternative detector architectures (two-stage, transformer-based, and vision-language grounding) with a decisive, measured justification for the detector actually deployed.

2. **A group-detection and O-space estimation method** (Objective 3) adapted specifically to this dataset's real constraints — no depth, no calibration, and no orientation ground truth at all — validated against a purpose-built, hand-labelled test set, with the validation **completed and its result reported honestly even though it falls below the project's own target** (Section 1.5 above; Methodology Section 3.3.1).

3. **A Behavioural Cloning model** (Objective 4) trained to predict a socially appropriate approach pose directly from non-expert demonstrations, tuned via a validation-only grid search rather than hand-picked defaults, and evaluated against both a naive statistical floor and a hand-engineered rule-based baseline on fully held-out test data.

4. **A rigorously investigated, and ultimately rejected, model-improvement study.** Rather than accepting the offline Behavioural Cloning model's initial accuracy as final, this project undertook a full diagnostic investigation — a re-segmented "v2" training dataset, a systematic 360-model hyperparameter search across three model families, and a directly measured "label-ambiguity floor" quantifying the true ceiling on achievable accuracy — that located the limiting factor precisely in the demonstration corpus rather than in model choice, and is reported as a disclosed, evidence-based rejection rather than a silently abandoned experiment (Methodology Section 3.5).

5. **A working, integrated robot system and a full experimental campaign**, comprising perception, group estimation, and either the rule-based or one of two learned policies, running live through Nav2 in a simulated environment purpose-built with ground-truth ready for scoring, and evaluated across a 60-trial experiment (three policies × two perception conditions × ten repeats) specifically designed so the rule-versus-learned comparison is a controlled, single-variable one rather than an offline number taken in isolation.

6. **A novel, non-saturating evaluation method for approach accuracy.** Where the project's originally-specified binary task-success metric saturated at the ceiling in the final experiment (every policy succeeding in every trial under the primary detector), a continuous, theory-derived metric was developed that scores how close the robot came to any of several ideal standing positions computed directly from F-formation geometry — itself the product of its own documented, three-stage internal debugging history rather than presented as correct on first attempt (Methodology Section 3.7.6).

7. **A disclosed, honestly reported record of significant engineering and measurement faults** found and corrected across the live system's development — including faults that had been silently invalidating earlier results (a collision metric measuring the robot's own chassis; a task-success metric scored against a stale perception target rather than ground truth) — reported as part of the methodology on the principle that a fault which changed a reported number is a methodological fact, not merely a debugging footnote (Methodology Section 3.6.5).

## 1.7 Alignment with the MSc Computer Science programme

This project sits within robotics and machine learning, two of the core research areas within Computer Science, and specifically at their intersection: it applies supervised machine learning — Behavioural Cloning, feature engineering, model selection, and hyperparameter tuning via a properly validation-isolated grid search and, subsequently, a systematic 360-model search — to a robotics perception-and-control problem: person detection, spatial clustering, and navigation via a modern ROS 2/Nav2 stack, evaluated with the rigour a Masters-level research project in Computer Science is expected to bring. That rigour is not asserted but demonstrated throughout: session-level data splitting to avoid information leakage between training and evaluation, held-out test evaluation performed only once per model family, small-sample statistical treatment grounded in the field's own methodological literature (Hoffman and Zhao, 2020) rather than applied silently, and a stated, honest accounting of where a chosen method's own limitations — dataset size, feature design, distributional shift, a re-specified validation criterion still not met — most plausibly explain a result, rather than treating an unfavourable finding as something to minimise or omit.

The project also draws directly on human-computer interaction and social-robotics research — F-formation theory, proxemics, human-aware navigation — to define what "success" should even mean for a robotics system whose ultimate audience is a human being rather than a benchmark score. This framing is treated as central to the dissertation's contribution rather than incidental context, since a technically accurate approach pose that a real bystander would find uncomfortable is not, in any meaningful sense, a successful one — a point this dissertation returns to explicitly as a stated limitation of its own evaluation methodology in Section 3.9. Taken together, the project's combination of applied machine learning, systems engineering, and human-centred evaluation criteria is representative of exactly the kind of interdisciplinary, rigorously evaluated work an MSc Computer Science research project is intended to demonstrate.

## 1.8 Dissertation structure

The remainder of this dissertation is organised as follows.

**Chapter 2 (Literature Review)** situates this project within the wider research on proxemics and F-formation theory, human-aware and socially aware robot navigation, computational group-structure detection, robot approach behaviour for both individuals and groups, Learning from Demonstration and Behavioural Cloning, the supervised-learning methods this project's own models implement, the supporting perception and platform literature, and the experimental-methodology and statistical literature grounding this project's small-sample evaluation design — thirty-four verified sources in total, each accompanied by an explicit statement of its relationship to this project's own work — closing with an explicit statement of the resulting research gap and a revisitation of the aims and objectives introduced above (Section 2.11), now read against the literature in full.

**Chapter 3 (Methodology)** sets out the project's overall research design and the reasoning behind every major methodological choice: why a quantitative comparative experiment rather than a qualitative study; how the dataset's own limitations shaped the perception, clustering, and validation approach; a full survey of alternative person-detection architectures and the measured justification for the one actually deployed; how the Behavioural Cloning model's target and features were designed and why; the full, disclosed account of the rejected "v2" model-improvement study and the systematic hyperparameter search that followed it; how the simulation environment, the twenty-two engineering faults found and corrected during its development, and the evaluation metrics were constructed to make the rule-versus-learned comparison a fair, single-variable one; and a critical discussion of every significant methodological trade-off the project made, stated as a trade rather than only an outcome.

**Chapter 4 (Implementation)** describes what was actually built to realise that methodology: the software architecture, toolchain, and engineering choices behind the ROS 2 system's packages and nodes, the concrete code-level realisation of the algorithms Chapter 3 justifies — clustering, approach-point selection, the rule-based policy's staged refinement, the learned models' technical implementation — and how the system was built, organised, and tested as a piece of software engineering distinct from the research design decisions that motivated it.

**Chapter 5 (Results & Discussion)** reports what happened when the resulting system was run: the offline comparison between the learned and rule-based policies against the held-out demonstration data, the full 60-trial live simulation results across both perception conditions, the F-formation slot-based accuracy analysis, and the Objective 3 validation result — discussed critically and explicitly linked back to the aims, objectives, and research questions set out in this chapter.

**Chapter 6 (Conclusion)** summarises what this project achieved against each of the four objectives and three research questions stated above, states its limitations plainly rather than generically, and sets out what a continuation of this work — grounded in the specific, quantified findings of Chapter 3's model-improvement study rather than a vague call for "more data" — should address next.
